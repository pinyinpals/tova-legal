How it works

Read it in three steps

  1. Set the scan language to Korean, aim the camera at the sign, and tap the shutter.
  2. Read the English with Revised Romanization above each block of Hangul, and tap to hear it spoken.
  3. Save names you will need again — a station, a street, a shop — to Favourites.

Where English runs out

Seoul's subway is signed in Korean and English, and most big stations add Chinese and Japanese. If you only ever ride the metro between tourist sights, you will cope without help.

The English thins out quickly after that. Shop fronts, restaurant doors, bus stops outside the centre, notices taped to a door, the sign on a toilet in a basement bar, anything in a smaller city — these are Hangul only. That is where a camera earns its place.

Tova reads the sign in one capture and shows the results in a panel at the bottom of the screen: the Revised Romanization above each block of Hangul and the English underneath. The romanisation is the part that matters when you have to match a sign to a map app, or ask a stranger for directions.

Real situations, step by step

Changing lines on the subway

  1. Follow 갈아타는 곳 (garataneun got), not the exit signs. Transfer signs are colour-coded by line and usually carry the line number, so you can follow the colour even before you read anything.
  2. Check the direction, not just the line. A platform sign reads like 시청 방면 (sicheong bangmyeon) — towards City Hall. Find the name of the end of the line, or the next station, on the sign and on your route.
  3. Scan the platform sign if the English is small or missing. The romanisation lets you match the Korean name to the one in your map app letter for letter.

Choosing the right exit number

  1. Get the exit number before you leave the train. Map apps and hotel directions give it as a number — "exit 3". Big stations have ten or more exits, some far apart.
  2. Look for the yellow 출구 signs and the number beside them. Near the fare gates there is usually a map listing the landmarks at each exit.
  3. If the landmark list is Korean only, capture it. You are looking for the name of your hotel, a department store or a street, and the romanisation above the Hangul is how you will recognise it.

Finding a toilet that is locked

  1. Look for 화장실 (hwajangsil) and the arrow. In older buildings the toilets are often in the stairwell or on a shared floor, not inside the restaurant.
  2. Check the door for 비밀번호 (bimilbeonho). A keypad means there is a code. It is usually printed on your receipt or taped up near the counter.
  3. Ask with the word, not a sentence. Saying "hwajangsil?" and pointing is understood everywhere.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving by the nearest exit. In a large station the wrong exit can put you across a main road or a long block from where you meant to be. Walk underground to the right number instead.
  • Reading 방면 as the station you are at. The name before 방면 is where the train is going, not where you are.
  • Treating romanisation as English spelling. "eo" is one vowel, close to the o in "hot", and "eu" is a short, unrounded u. Tap to listen before you say a name to a taxi driver.
  • Expecting one spelling everywhere. Older signs and some shop names use other romanisation systems, so Busan may appear as Pusan. The Hangul does not change; match on that.
  • Relying on the live view for handwritten notices. A note taped to a door is often handwritten. Tap the shutter — the capture path uses handwriting recognition that the live scan does not.

Signs and menus use the same alphabet

Once you can match a few station names, a menu gets less intimidating — it is the same alphabet in the same syllable blocks. The Korean menu guide covers the words for dish types, cooking methods and spice, and how ordering by the portion works at a BBQ.

If you are heading on to Japan or China, the camera works the same way for Japanese signs with romaji and Chinese street signs with pinyin.

Common questions

About this guide

What's the best app to translate Korean signs?

Tova Translate reads Korean from your camera and shows Revised Romanization above each block of Hangul with the English underneath, plus tap-to-listen audio. It works offline and is free to download on iPhone, iPad and Android.

Does it romanise Hangul?

Yes. Tova uses Revised Romanization, the official system in South Korea and the one on most road and subway signs, so what you read in the app matches what you see on the street.

Will it work without a data connection in Korea?

Yes. When there is no signal Tova switches to on-device translation automatically, and Korean results still show romanisation. Download the free offline pack before you go under Settings, Offline packs.

Do I need a translator for the Seoul subway?

Less than elsewhere, because the Seoul subway is signed in English too. It helps most with exit landmark maps, platform notices, bus stops and anything outside the station.

Why does the same place have two spellings?

Korea changed its official romanisation in 2000. Older signs and some brand names still use earlier spellings, such as Pusan for Busan. The Hangul is the same in both cases, so match on that.

How many languages does Tova cover beyond Korean?

Translation covers 118 languages, and the camera reads 11 writing systems including Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Thai, Arabic and Hebrew.
