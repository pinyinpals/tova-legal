Hangul is easier than it looks, the menu is harder

Korean is written in Hangul, an alphabet of 24 letters stacked into syllable blocks. You could learn to sound it out on the flight. The problem is that sounding out 된장찌개 as doenjang-jjigae still does not tell you it is a fermented soybean stew, or that it arrives bubbling in a stone pot.

Tova does both halves. The camera reads the whole menu in one capture, and the results appear in a panel at the bottom of the screen: the Revised Romanization sits above each block of Hangul, the English underneath. You get the meaning, and you get something you can say out loud.

Like Chinese, most Korean dish names follow a pattern: main ingredient + cooking method, or ingredient + dish type. 돼지갈비 is pork (돼지) ribs (갈비). 김치찌개 is kimchi (김치) stew (찌개). 오징어볶음 is squid (오징어) stir-fried (볶음). Learn the endings in the tables below and a lot of the menu reads itself.

Real situations, step by step

Korean BBQ, where you order by the portion

  1. Scan the menu on the wall or the table. BBQ menus are short: a list of cuts, a price per 인분 (portion), then stews, rice and drinks at the bottom.
  2. Order by the portion, not by the plate. Many BBQ places ask for at least two portions of meat, even for one person. Point at the cut and hold up fingers, or say 삼겹살 2인분 주세요 (samgyeopsal i-inbun juseyo) — two portions of pork belly, please.
  3. Let the staff start the grill. They will often cut and turn the meat for you. The side dishes arrive unasked and are part of the meal.
  4. Finish with rice or a stew. Ordering 공기밥 or a 된장찌개 (doenjang-jjigae, soybean paste stew) at the end is normal. Pay at the counter on the way out; there is no tipping.

A kiosk or table tablet

  1. Look for a language button first. Many kiosks have one. Many do not, and the ones that do often translate only the top-level menu.
  2. Capture the screen with Tova. Screens reflect, so tilt the phone slightly to lose the glare before you tap the shutter.
  3. Save what you picked. If the kiosk times out, you can find the dish again from Favourites instead of starting over.

A pojangmacha (street tent bar)

  1. Expect a handwritten board or none at all. Tent bars often list a handful of dishes in marker pen. Tap the shutter rather than relying on the live view, which is tuned for print — see the handwriting guide for why.
  2. Order drinks first. Soju and a shared dish — 떡볶이 (tteokbokki, rice cakes in chilli sauce) or 어묵 (eomuk, fish cake skewers) — is the usual shape of an order.
  3. Pay in cash if you can. Not every stall takes cards.

What to say

Do not trust a phone with a severe allergy. Machine translation can drop a negation, and fish sauce or salted shrimp turn up in places you would not expect — including most kimchi. If a reaction would send you to hospital, carry a card written by a native speaker and show it.

Common mistakes

  • Ordering one portion of BBQ for the table. A 인분 is roughly one person's share, and many places will not serve fewer than two.
  • Assuming red means spicy and white means safe. 백김치 (baek-gimchi, white kimchi) is mild, but plenty of pale soups get chilli at the table, and 짬뽕 (jjamppong) is a seafood noodle soup that is very hot.
  • Ordering side dishes. 반찬 come free with the meal and are usually refilled if you ask. You do not need to find them on the menu.
  • Reading romanisation as English. "eo" is one vowel, close to the o in "hot", not "e-o". The tap-to-listen button is quicker than guessing.
  • Waiting for the bill at the table. In most casual restaurants you take the receipt to the counter and pay there.

Why the romanisation matters

A plain translation tells you 순두부찌개 is soft tofu stew. The romanisation, sundubu-jjigae, is what lets you ask for it — and what lets you spot it again tomorrow on a different menu with no English at all.

The same one-capture scan reads the street outside too: Korean signs use the same alphabet, and station names are a good place to practise. If your trip continues elsewhere, the approach carries over to Chinese menus with pinyin and Japanese menus with romaji.
