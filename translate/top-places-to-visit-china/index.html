Top 10 places to visit in China, and how to book them

Last updated: November 2026

Five landscapes and five pieces of history, from the sandstone pillars of Zhangjiajie to the painted caves of Dunhuang. For each one: where it is, how a foreign visitor books it, how long to give it and the Chinese you’ll see at the gate, with a map, a booking table and the best season for every place.

Zhangjiajie: the stone pillars of Wulingyuan

Thousands of sandstone pillars, some over two hundred metres tall, rise out of forest in north-west Hunan. The park inside the Wulingyuan Scenic Area is the one most visitors mean by Zhangjiajie, and one of its pillars was renamed after the film Avatar, whose floating mountains it resembles. Eco-buses run between the trailheads, and the Bailong Elevator and the Tianzi Mountain cableway lift you onto the ridges for an extra fare.

The park ticket covers several days, which is how the park is meant to be seen: stay in Wulingyuan town at the gate rather than in Zhangjiajie city, and go out early before the tour groups arrive. Fog is common and can close in a view for hours, so a spare day helps.

Tianmen Mountain and the Grand Canyon glass bridge are separate parks with their own tickets. The long cableway up Tianmen Mountain from the city stopped carrying passengers in October 2026; in November 2026 visitors were going up by a shorter cableway to Tianmen Cave and escalators through the mountain, so check the park’s notices before you plan around it.

Book
Ahead in busy seasons, through the park’s official mini program. Ticket windows at the gates have staff who help in English.
Getting there
High-speed train to Zhangjiajie West, then about an hour by road to Wulingyuan town.
Time needed
Two to three days, plus a day each for Tianmen Mountain and the glass bridge.
Effort
High: steep stone steps and long walks between viewpoints. The elevator and cableways save the climbs.
Best time
April to June and September to early November.

The Great Wall at Mutianyu, Badaling or Jinshanling

The stretches north of Beijing are the Ming dynasty wall most people picture, running along ridges for as far as you can see. Which section you choose matters more than anything else about the day.

Mutianyu is restored, wooded and less crowded than Badaling, with a cable car, a chairlift and a toboggan down. Badaling is the most visited section and the easiest to reach, with its own station on the high-speed line from Beijing to Zhangjiakou. Jinshanling, across the border in Hebei, is partly unrestored and much quieter; it’s a long day from the city and the best of the three for walking. Simatai is entered through Gubei Water Town and is known for evening visits.

According to the Beijing government’s visitor pages, Mutianyu and Badaling don’t need a booking and sell tickets on site; Badaling has English-speaking ticket windows and machines that take foreign cards. Go early, and avoid weekends and holidays at Badaling.

Book
Not needed at Mutianyu or Badaling: buy at the gate. Badaling has English-speaking windows that take foreign cards.
Getting there
Badaling by high-speed train from Beijing Qinghe; Mutianyu and Jinshanling by road.
Time needed
A full day including the journey.
Effort
Medium to high: the wall is steep in places. A cable car does the climb at Mutianyu.
Best time
April to May and September to October.

The Forbidden City and the Temple of Heaven, Beijing

The palace of the Ming and Qing emperors is the largest collection of old wooden buildings in China, a walk of courtyard after courtyard from the Meridian Gate in the south to the Gate of Divine Might in the north. The Temple of Heaven, a short metro ride south, is where the emperors prayed for good harvests; its round blue-roofed hall is one of the best-known buildings in the country, and the park around it fills with people exercising and singing in the early morning.

The Palace Museum sells tickets only in advance. Sales open at 20:00 Beijing time seven days ahead on its official website, which has an English version where you book with your passport and an email address; nothing is sold on the day, and weekends and holidays go fast. Enter at the Meridian Gate with the same passport. If the booking won’t go through, the museum’s help line, 400-950-1925, answers in English. The Temple of Heaven books one to seven days ahead through its official WeChat account, and sells at the ticket office on the day if it isn’t full; the Beijing government says no third-party seller is authorised for it.

  • Book: the Forbidden City on the Palace Museum’s English booking site. The Treasure Gallery and the Clock Gallery are booked the same way, for a small extra fee.
  • Tip: climb the hill in Jingshan Park, opposite the north gate, for the view back over the palace roofs.
Book
Required. Sales open at 20:00 Beijing time, seven days ahead, on the English website. No tickets on the day.
Closed
Mondays, except national holidays. The Temple of Heaven’s halls also close on Mondays.
Time needed
Three to four hours for the palace, an hour or two for the Temple of Heaven.
Effort
Medium: three or four kilometres on flat stone, with little shade.
Best time
April to May and September to October, on a weekday.

The Terracotta Army and the city wall, Xi’an

Thousands of life-size clay soldiers, each with its own face, were buried around 210 BC to guard the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of a unified China. Pit 1, the size of an aircraft hangar, is the one in the photographs; pits 2 and 3 and the bronze chariots in the museum are worth the extra hour.

The museum is in Lintong, about 40 km east of central Xi’an. Every visitor needs a real-name booking with a timed entry, made on the museum’s official website or its WeChat channels, and a passport is accepted. Many sellers resell tickets and tours, so check that you’re buying the official ticket or a tour that includes it.

Back in the city, the Ming city wall is complete all the way round. Hire a bike on top and ride the 13.7 km loop, ideally late in the afternoon.

  • Book: on the museum’s official ticketing page, and arrive half an hour before your slot.
Book
Required for everyone, with a timed entry. Passports are accepted, and the museum’s overseas booking takes Visa and Mastercard.
Getting there
High-speed train to Xi’an North, then about an hour by bus or taxi to Lintong.
Time needed
Half a day for the army, two to three hours for the city wall.
Effort
Low to medium: walking between the pits and the museum halls.
Best time
March to May and September to November.

The Li River and Yangshuo, Guangxi

Between Guilin and Yangshuo the Li River winds through hundreds of steep green karst peaks, the landscape of countless scroll paintings. Guilin’s karst was added to the South China Karst World Heritage Site in 2014. The stretch at Xingping is the scene printed on the back of the 20-yuan note.

The classic day is a boat downriver from Guilin to Yangshuo, about four hours, then a few days in Yangshuo itself: cycling between the peaks, a bamboo raft on the smaller Yulong River, and the walk or raft at Xingping. Yangshuo has its own station on the high-speed line, so you can arrive by train and do the river as a day trip from there.

Book
A day or more ahead for cruises, in the name on your passport. You collect the ticket at the pier.
Getting there
High-speed train or flight to Guilin, or train straight to Yangshuo.
Time needed
Two to three days.
Effort
Low: the boat does the work. Cycling around Yangshuo is flat.
Best time
April to October.

Huangshan, the Yellow Mountain, Anhui

Granite peaks, pines growing out of bare rock and, on the right morning, a sea of cloud below you: Huangshan is the mountain of Chinese landscape painting and one of the few World Heritage Sites listed for both its nature and its culture. The paths are stone steps, thousands of them, and cable cars carry you most of the way up from three sides.

The way to see it is to sleep on the summit and get up for the sunrise. Hotels at the top cost more than those in town, but they put you on the peaks before the day visitors arrive. Every visit needs a booking with a time slot, made in advance; the official ticket can be bought as a multi-entry ticket valid for three days, and the cable cars are paid separately.

Book
Required, with a time slot, through the official mini program; at least three days ahead around holidays.
Getting there
High-speed train to Huangshan North, then about two hours by bus to the foot of the mountain.
Time needed
Two days, with a night on the summit.
Effort
Very high: thousands of stone steps. Cable cars from three sides take most of the climb.
Best time
April to May and September to November.

West Lake, Hangzhou

West Lake has been shaped by people for more than a thousand years: causeways built by poet-governors, pagodas on the hills, tea plantations behind. Walking or cycling the Su Causeway and around to the Broken Bridge is free, as is the lake itself, and boats cross to the islands for a fare.

In November 2025 the Feilaifeng scenic area around Lingyin Temple, with its Buddhist carvings in the rock, became free to enter too. Go on a weekday if you can: weekends and holidays around the Broken Bridge are shoulder to shoulder.

Book
Not needed: the lake is free. Boats and paid sights sell at the gate.
Getting there
A short high-speed train ride from Shanghai to Hangzhou, then the metro.
Time needed
One to two days.
Effort
Low: flat paths and causeways, with shared bikes everywhere.
Best time
March to April and September to November, on a weekday.

The classical gardens of Suzhou

Suzhou’s scholars and officials built gardens that fit hills, lakes and forests into a city block, with rocks, pavilions and windows framing each view like a painting. Nine of them are on the World Heritage list. The Humble Administrator’s Garden is the largest and busiest; the Lingering Garden and the small Master-of-the-Nets Garden are quieter and, for many visitors, better.

The gardens book under your real name and the popular ones sell out on busy days, so book ahead and arrive at opening time, before the tour groups. Leave time for Pingjiang Road, a canal street of tea houses where you can hear pingtan, Suzhou’s sung storytelling.

Book
Ahead in busy seasons for the Humble Administrator’s Garden, under your name. Bring the original passport.
Getting there
About half an hour from Shanghai by high-speed train.
Time needed
A day for two or three gardens.
Effort
Low.
Best time
March to May and September to November. Go at opening time.

The Mogao Caves, Dunhuang

On the edge of the desert at Dunhuang, Buddhist monks and patrons carved hundreds of caves into a cliff between the 4th and 14th centuries and painted them from floor to ceiling. They’re the richest store of Buddhist art on the Silk Road, and the most fragile site on this list.

To protect the paintings, visitor numbers are tightly limited. A visit starts at the Digital Exhibition Center outside town with two films, then a shuttle takes you to the cliff for a guided tour of a set of caves; which ones you see varies. In the busy months a cheaper ticket with fewer caves is sometimes sold once the full tickets are gone. Book on the official channel as early as you can; if that isn’t possible, book a tour that includes the ticket. Expect photography to be banned inside the caves.

Book
Required, with a tight daily quota; sales open about a month ahead. Online booking with a foreign passport isn’t reliable, so a tour that includes the ticket is the safe route.
Getting there
Fly to Dunhuang, or take a long train along the Hexi Corridor.
Time needed
Half a day for the caves, two to three days for Dunhuang.
Effort
Low to medium: some stairs between the caves.
Best time
May to October, booked early.

Jiuzhaigou, Sichuan

A valley of lakes so clear and blue-green they look painted, linked by waterfalls and boardwalks, in the mountains of northern Sichuan. An earthquake in 2017 damaged parts of it, including Nuorilang Falls; the park reopened fully in September 2021.

It’s much easier to reach than it used to be. Since August 2024 high-speed trains run from Chengdu East to Huanglong Jiuzhai station in under two hours, and buses take about two more to the park. Tickets are sold only in advance, under your real name; foreign visitors book with a passport and scan it at the gate, and the daily limit of 41,000 can be reached in October. The park bus that links the lakes is part of the visit. The station sits at nearly 3,000 m, so take the first day slowly.

Book
Required, online only, with a daily limit of 41,000. Foreigners book with a passport, scan it at the gate and can pay with foreign cards.
Getting there
High-speed train from Chengdu East to Huanglong Jiuzhai, then about two hours by bus.
Time needed
A full day in the park, two with Huanglong.
Effort
Medium: boardwalks and buses, but at altitude. The station is at nearly 3,000 m.
Best time
May to June and September to November; October is the most crowded.

Three routes that link them

China is big, and the trains are the way to see a lot of it without wasting days. These three routes are the coloured lines on the map above; each takes one to two weeks and works in either direction. Check times and book on 12306, the official railway site, which has an English version.

  • The classic loop (two weeks): Beijing for the Forbidden City and the Great Wall, high-speed train to Xi’an for the Terracotta Army, then on to Zhangjiajie, and finish at Guilin and Yangshuo. Fly on from Guilin or carry on to Guangzhou and Hong Kong.
  • The east (one week): start in Shanghai for the Bund and the old lanes, then Suzhou’s gardens, Hangzhou’s West Lake and up into the mountains at Huangshan. Every leg is a short train ride.
  • The west (ten days): Chengdu for the pandas, high-speed train to Jiuzhaigou, then fly north to Dunhuang for the Mogao Caves and the desert.

Five more worth the trip

  • Chengdu Research Base of Giant Panda Breeding (成都大熊猫繁育研究基地): book up to 14 days ahead with your passport and go at opening time, when the pandas are awake and feeding. Panda Base tickets on Trip.com, one of the base’s authorised sellers.
  • Leshan Giant Buddha (乐山大佛): a 71-metre Buddha carved into a riverside cliff, about an hour from Chengdu by train. The stairway down beside it can mean a long queue; a boat gives you the whole figure from the river.
  • Potala Palace, Lhasa (布达拉宫): foreign visitors to Tibet need a Tibet Travel Permit, arranged through a licensed Tibet travel agency as part of a guided tour, so this is a trip to plan through an agency rather than alone.
  • Pingyao Ancient City (平遥古城): a walled Ming and Qing town in Shanxi, home of China’s first draft banks. Walking the streets is free; one combined ticket covers the wall and the main buildings.
  • Lijiang Old Town (丽江古城): canals, wooden houses and the Naxi people’s culture in Yunnan, below Jade Dragon Snow Mountain. Busy and commercial at holidays; nearby Shuhe is quieter.

Booking, queues and signs at every site

Most big sights in China are booked under your real name and checked against your passport at the gate, so book with the passport you’ll be carrying and keep it on you. Many have a daily limit and a weekly closing day, usually Monday for museums. Domestic booking happens mostly in WeChat mini programs; where a site has an English or passport route, we’ve linked it above.

Avoid the big holidays if you possibly can: the National Day week from 1 to 7 October above all, then the May Day holiday at the start of May and the Spring Festival. Sites reach their limits, and trains sell out weeks ahead.

Common questions

About this guide

What are the best places to visit in China for a first trip?

The Great Wall and the Forbidden City in Beijing, the Terracotta Army in Xi’an, the Li River at Guilin and Yangshuo, and Zhangjiajie’s sandstone pillars are the five most people put first. All five link up by high-speed train, so they make a two-week route.

Can foreigners book Forbidden City tickets?

Yes. The Palace Museum’s official website has an English version where you book with your passport. Tickets go on sale seven days ahead, nothing is sold at the gate, and the museum is closed on Mondays except national holidays. Bring the same passport and enter at the Meridian Gate.

How many days do you need in Zhangjiajie?

Two to three days for the core Wulingyuan park, with a night in Wulingyuan town at the park gate. Add a day each for Tianmen Mountain and the Grand Canyon glass bridge, which are separate parks with their own tickets.

Is the Tianmen Mountain cable car running?

Not the long one from the city. It stopped carrying passengers in October 2026, and no reopening date had been announced in November 2026. Visitors were going up by a shorter cableway to Tianmen Cave and then escalators through the mountain. Check the park’s official notices before you go.

Do I need to book Chinese attractions in advance?

For most of the big ones, yes. The Forbidden City, the Terracotta Army, Jiuzhaigou and the Mogao Caves all need a booking made in advance under your passport, and there is a daily limit on visitors. The Great Wall at Mutianyu and Badaling and the lake at Hangzhou are exceptions.

When is the worst time to visit China’s famous sights?

The National Day holiday from 1 to 7 October, the May Day holiday at the start of May and the Spring Festival holiday. Most of China travels then, trains sell out and many sites reach their daily limit.
