A Japanese menu is built from a few dozen words

Japanese menus look harder than they are because they mix three scripts on one line: kanji for the dish, hiragana for the grammar, katakana for anything borrowed from another language. カレーライス is curry rice. 豚カツ定食 is a pork cutlet set meal. Once you can spot the pieces, a dish name usually reads as ingredient + cooking method + dish type.

You will not learn to read Japanese from a menu. You can learn the forty or fifty words that decide whether you get noodles or rice, raw or fried, one bowl or a whole tray. The tables below are those words, grouped the way they turn up on a real menu.

If the scripts themselves are the problem, our guide to reading Japanese with romaji explains hiragana, katakana and kanji, and why the same kanji is read differently in different words.

At a ticket machine restaurant

Many ramen, soba and gyūdon shops have no menu at the table and no one taking orders. You pay at a machine by the door first. The buttons are small, crowded and usually only in Japanese.

  1. Scan the machine before you put money in. Set Tova to Japanese and tap the shutter on the whole button panel. The panel at the bottom of the camera screen shows each button's text with romaji above the characters and the English underneath. If the glare off the plastic ruins the read, step to one side and try again.
  2. Look at the top-left button. On most machines that is the house speciality, and the bigger buttons are the dishes the shop wants you to buy.
  3. Insert cash first, then press. Many machines only take cash, and plenty refuse ¥10,000 notes. Newer ones take IC transit cards.
  4. Take the ticket and your change, and hand the ticket over. Put it on the counter or give it to staff when you sit.
  5. Be ready for one question. Ramen shops often ask about noodle firmness: 硬め katame (firm), 普通 futsū (normal), 柔らかめ yawarakame (soft). 普通 is always a safe answer.

At an izakaya

  1. Expect the お通し. A small dish arrives that you did not order. It is the table charge, and it is normal.
  2. Capture the whole page in one go. Izakaya menus are long, often handwritten on a board or on paper strips on the wall. For handwriting, tap the shutter rather than relying on the live scan — the capture uses Apple's handwriting recognition. Neat print reads well; hurried brush strokes can still fail, and when they do, ask for the printed menu.
  3. Call staff when you are ready. Raise a hand and say すみません (sumimasen). Many places have a call button on the table instead.
  4. Order a few dishes at a time. Izakaya food is shared and comes as it is ready, so there is no need to order everything at once.
  5. Ask for お会計 at the end. Some places bring the bill to the table; others want you to pay at the till by the door.

Saying what you cannot eat

Do not trust a phone with a severe allergy. Machine translation can drop a negation, and dashi stock made from fish goes into far more dishes than it looks. If a reaction would put you in hospital, carry a card written by a native speaker and show it. Many chain restaurants also have an allergen chart — ask for アレルギー表 (arerugī hyō).

Common mistakes

  • Reading 並 as small. It means regular. The small size, if there is one, is 小盛り (komori).
  • Adding up a 税抜 menu. Prices before tax will come to about ten per cent more at the till.
  • Treating the お通し as a mistake. It is a cover charge. Sending it back does not remove it from the bill.
  • Assuming 生 means fresh. On food it means raw. On beer it means draught.
  • Expecting romaji with no signal. Tova still translates offline, but the romaji for kanji needs a connection, because the right reading depends on the words around it. In a basement bar with no reception you may get the English without the reading.
  • Asking for a 替え玉 after finishing the soup. The extra noodles go into your leftover broth. Drink it first and you have nothing to put them in.

Why the romaji matters at the counter

A translation tells you 親子丼 is chicken and egg on rice. It does not tell you how to ask for it. With oyakodon printed above the characters you can say the name, and the person behind the counter hears a word they know instead of watching you hunt for a picture.

What's the best app to translate a Japanese menu with romaji?

Tova Translate reads a Japanese menu from your camera and shows romaji above each character in a panel at the bottom of the screen, with the English translation underneath and tap-to-listen audio. It is free to download on iPhone and iPad, and it captures a whole page in one shot.

Does it put romaji above kanji as well as kana?

Yes, when you have a connection. Kanji readings depend on the word they sit in, so Tova works them out from the whole phrase rather than one character at a time. Offline you still get the translation, but the romaji for kanji may be missing.

Can I use it offline in Japan?

Yes. When Tova cannot reach its servers it falls back to on-device translation, so basements, trains and rural restaurants still get an English reading. Download the Japanese offline pack first in Settings, Offline packs. The one thing you may lose offline is the romaji for kanji.

How do I use a Japanese ticket machine?

Scan the button panel first so you know what each button is, insert cash, press the dish you want, then take the ticket and your change and hand the ticket to staff. The top-left button is usually the house speciality.

Does it handle handwritten or chalkboard menus?

Often. Tap the shutter rather than relying on the live scan, because the capture uses Apple's handwriting recognition. Neat handwriting reads well. Very cursive or decorative brush writing can still fail, and a printed menu is the better option when it does.

What is the small dish I did not order at an izakaya?

It is the otōshi, a small starter that works as a table charge. It is normal in izakaya and will appear on the bill whether or not you eat it.
