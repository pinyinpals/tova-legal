A Chinese menu is a formula, not a list

Most dish names are built the same way: cooking method + main ingredient + cut or flavour. 红烧牛肉 is red-braised (红烧) beef (牛肉). 清蒸鱼 is plain-steamed (清蒸) fish (鱼). 麻辣豆腐 is numbing-spicy (麻辣) tofu (豆腐).

Learn about forty characters and you stop reading dish names and start reading recipes. You will not know exactly what arrives, but you will know whether it is fried or steamed, whether it is pork or fish, and whether it will hurt.

Saying what you cannot eat

Do not trust a phone with a severe allergy. Machine translation drops negations, kitchens cross-contaminate, and 花生油 (peanut oil) is a default cooking fat in much of China. If a reaction would send you to hospital, carry a chef's card written by a native speaker and show it — do not rely on a screen, an app, or this page.

我对花生过敏wǒ duì huāshēng guòmǐnI am allergic to peanuts

Ordering when you cannot say any of it

  1. Scan the whole page first, not one line. Tova reads the entire menu in one capture and puts the pinyin above each character, so you can see the shape of the menu before you commit to a dish. The same one-capture scan works on Korean menus and Japanese menus.
  2. Point at the characters, not at your phone. Staff read their own menu far faster than they read your screen. Find the dish, then point at the paper.
  3. Use the number, if there is one. Most Chinese menus number their dishes. Saying the number in English and holding up fingers works almost everywhere.
  4. Show, do not tell, for anything that matters. Dietary restrictions go on a card or a screen in Chinese characters, held up — not spoken in approximate pinyin.
  5. Photograph the menu before you leave. You will want the name of whatever was good, and it will be gone the moment you walk out.

Why the readings matter at a table

A translation tells you 宫保鸡丁 is Kung Pao chicken. It does not tell you how to say it, so you point, and the exchange ends there.

With gōng bǎo jī dīng printed above the characters you can attempt the name out loud. You will get the tones wrong. It does not matter — the staff will correct you, and that is a conversation rather than a transaction. It is also how the characters start to stick, so the second week of a trip is easier than the first. That is the whole case for a pinyin camera app over a plain translator.
