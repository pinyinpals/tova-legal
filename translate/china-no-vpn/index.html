Why your translation app stops working when you land

Mainland China filters international internet traffic, and Google's services are on the wrong side of that filter. Google Translate — the app most travellers already have — cannot reach its servers from a Chinese network. Google ran a China-specific version at translate.google.cn for years, but shut it down in October 2022, so the old workaround is gone too.

What makes this worse than a normal outage is how it fails. The app opens. The camera turns on. You point it at a menu and nothing happens, or the text freezes half-translated. There is no error telling you the country you are standing in is the problem, so most people assume their phone is broken or the hotel Wi-Fi is slow, and keep retrying at the exact moment they needed an answer.

The fix is not a single app. It is deciding, before you fly, which of your tools work on the device itself — with the aeroplane in flight mode, no SIM, no Wi-Fi — because that is the only capability the filter cannot touch.

Set it up before you fly

Do all of this on your home Wi-Fi. Every step needs a connection that mainland China will not give you.

  1. Download offline language packs in every translation app you plan to use. In Tova, Settings → Offline packs. In Apple Translate, Settings → Translate → Downloaded Languages. In Google Translate, the download arrow next to Chinese.
  2. Test each one in flight mode before you leave the house. Turn on aeroplane mode, then actually translate something. An app that shows a spinner in your kitchen will show the same spinner in Chengdu.
  3. Photograph the things you will need offline — your hotel booking with the Chinese address, your visa page, the Chinese name of anywhere you plan to go. A screenshot survives a dead network.
  4. Write down your hotel address in Chinese characters and keep it on paper. Every taxi conversation starts there, and paper does not run out of battery.
  5. Install anything you might want later, now. The App Store itself is reachable in China, but downloads can be slow and some apps are unavailable in the Chinese storefront.

Roaming and eSIMs: the quiet workaround

If you keep your home SIM active and pay for international roaming, your data is usually routed back through your home carrier's network before it reaches the internet. In practice that means you are browsing as if you were still at home, and the services blocked on local networks often load normally.

The same is true of most travel eSIMs sold for China, which is why they are popular with business travellers. It is not a guarantee — some route locally, and carriers change their arrangements — so check with your provider rather than assuming, and never make roaming your only plan. Roaming data is also expensive enough that camera translation of a long menu is a real cost.

A local Chinese SIM is the opposite: cheap, fast, and filtered exactly like every other domestic connection.

If you are already in China with nothing downloaded

Not ideal, but not hopeless.

  • Apple Translate is already on your iPhone and is reachable. It will not give you pinyin, but it will give you meaning.
  • The App Store works. You can still install an app that translates on-device — you just cannot install one that needs Google.
  • Hotel Wi-Fi is filtered too. It is the same internet. A VPN you install after arriving is also likely to be blocked; they have to be set up beforehand.
  • Ask. Staff at any hotel above budget level generally read some English, and pointing at a phone screen works better than you would expect.

Why pinyin matters more than you think

Most translation apps give you the meaning and stop. That is enough to order lunch, and not enough to say the name of the dish, ask where the bathroom is, or read the same character again tomorrow and recognise it.

Pinyin — the romanisation above each character — turns a translation into something you can pronounce out loud. In a country where the gap between reading and speaking is the whole problem, that is the difference between pointing at a phone and having a conversation. Tova prints it above every character; Pleco gives it in dictionary lookups; the mainstream translators mostly do not.
